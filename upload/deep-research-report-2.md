# Town Information  
**Chongoyape (Lambayeque)** – The town is **Chongoyape** (capital of its district), in Chiclayo Province, Lambayeque Region of Peru.  It lies in the coastal **Lambayeque** department, about 248 m above sea level and ≈60 km east of Chiclayo city.  Founded in 1825 (originally Santa Catalina de Chongoyape), the district became famous as *“tierra de los ricos bizcochuelos”* (“land of rich sponge cakes”). Chongoyape’s economy is largely agricultural, but locally it is best known for sweet specialties: traditionally-prepared **bizcochuelos** and **shurumbo** soup, and it gave its name to the Chongoyapana yaraví.  Lambayeque cuisine itself is rich in sweets (e.g. King Kong candies, King Kong cakes from Ferreñafe) as well as influ­ences from Spanish colonial baking (alfajores, arroz con leche) and local Andean ingredients.

# Research Summary  
Traditional Peruvian *bizcochuelos* are essentially sponge cakes made by whipping eggs and sugar into a large foam and folding in flour.  They have Spanish-French origins (akin to genoise/chiffon cakes) and became integrated into local cuisine.  In Lambayeque (and coastally), baking was influenced by colonial practices and available ingredients: wheat flour from Spain, sugarcane, and local starches like potato chuño.  For example, some classic Peruvian recipes use **chuño** (freeze-dried potato starch) in place of or alongside flour.  An *“El Comercio”* recipe for **bizcochuelo de chuño** uses 6 eggs, 1 cup sugar and 2 cups chuño.  Whether Valera uses chuño is unknown, but sources note “harinas especiales” (special flours) in their recipe, so a mix of wheat and starch is plausible.

Modern *bizcochuelo* recipes (e.g. RPP Noticias) often use equal weights of sugar and flour (200 g each) with 8 eggs, whipped at high speed to triple volume, then baked 30–40 minutes at ~180 °C.  Family tradition likely follows similar techniques: *“El sabor se mantiene por la calidad de producto… preparado todo a base de huevos frescos”*.  The thesis on Bizcochuelos Valera confirms they use **traditional wood-fired ovens** for color/flavor, and no fat or exotic agents – just eggs, sugar, and high-quality flours (possibly including locally-sourced starch).  The “secret” is likely in the proportions and beating method.  A referenced base recipe (Flo Braker’s Genoise) uses separated eggs plus a bit of liquid for extra volume, but on a small scale; whether Valera’s artisans separate yolks/whites or whip whole eggs isn’t known, but whipping to *“punto cinta”* (ribbon stage) is key.

In short, the Valera bizcochuelos align with classic sponge technique:  
- **Egg foam**: whole eggs (or whites/yolks) beaten until very voluminous (per [75]) in place of chemical leaveners.  
- **Flour/starch**: sifted in and folded gently to preserve air. Some Peruvian sponges use potato starch.  
- **Flavoring**: a bit of vanilla or citrus zest is common (not advertised on the label, but small amounts likely to enhance aroma).  
- **Baking**: moderate oven heat (180–200 °C) in flat pans, to avoid hard crust (the Valera cakes have a soft top). The wood oven likely runs around these temps.  

No Chinese, Italian or other immigrant ingredients directly appear; the technique is essentially European/peruvian. Chinese-Peruvian (chifa) influence is more evident in savory buns and fried bread, not in a traditional bizcochuelo. Italian immigrants brought panettone-type breads, but bizcochuelos were already local by 1913. Overall the product reflects Spanish colonial confectionery adapted to northern Peruvian ingredients and conditions.

# Replicated Recipe  

**Yield:** One rectangular sponge cake (~24×20 cm) yielding 16 pieces.  

**Ingredients:**  
- 8 large eggs (room temp)  
- 200 g granulated sugar  
- 200 g all-purpose wheat flour (harina 0000)  
- 100 g potato starch or corn starch (chuño or cornstarch, optional)  
- 10 g (≈2 tsp) baking powder (for reliability)  
- 1 tsp vanilla extract (or 1 tsp lemon zest + ½ tsp vanilla)  
- Pinch of salt  

**Equipment:** Cake pan ~24×20 cm, mixer, spatula.  

**Method:**  
1. Preheat oven to **180 °C**.  Grease and line (or flour) the cake pan.  
2. In a clean bowl, **whip the eggs and sugar** on high speed until the mixture is **pale, thick and triples in volume** (about 5–8 minutes). It should form a ribbon when drizzled.  
3. *Option A (whole eggs):* Proceed with this foam. *Option B (separate eggs):* Alternatively, you can separate yolks and whites: beat yolks with sugar first, then whip whites to firm peaks and fold in (this often gives even more volume).  

4. **Sift together** the flour, starch, baking powder, and salt.  
5. Gently **fold** the dry mix into the egg foam in batches, using wide sweeping motions to avoid deflating it.  If using Option B, first fold a third of the whipped whites to lighten the yolk mix, then add the rest. Add vanilla/zest at this stage.  
6. Once just combined, pour the batter into the prepared pan. Tap the pan lightly on the counter to release any large air bubbles.  

7. **Bake** in the preheated oven for ~**30–40 minutes**, or until a toothpick inserted center comes out clean. (Time may vary: start checking at 25 minutes. The cake should be risen, golden, and spring back lightly.)  
8. Immediately remove from oven and let **cool completely in pan** to set the structure. Then unmold and let cool on a rack.  

9. **Slice:** Cut the cooled cake into **16 equal pieces** (4×4 grid). Aim for uniform rectangles ~6–7 cm wide. This matches the product’s packaging count.  

**Tips:** Use fresh, room-temp eggs for maximum volume. Don’t overfold: mix just until no streaks of flour remain. Baking powder is optional (just 10 g here) since the main lift comes from eggs. The resulting cake should be light, spongy and slightly moist. For authenticity, one could bake in a wood-fired oven or use a stone base to mimic the traditional method.  

**Adjustments:** For a softer crumb, you can substitute 50–100% of the flour with potato starch (chuño) or add 1–2 tbsp of oil/yogurt as in chiffon cakes (modern variant). If your oven runs hot, reduce to 170 °C. For home baking, use a 20×25 cm pan and bake ~35–40 min; thinly slice to 16 rectangles. The labeled Valera bizcochuelos contain no visible frosting or toppings, so serve plain or with a side of **manjar blanco** (dulce de leche) if desired (since the shop also sells “bizcotelas” filled with manjar).
